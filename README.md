# Bjerk Builders | Moving Margin Control Upstream

### A data-driven operating model linking pre-award decisions to $940K in projected annual margin recovery potential

Bjerk Builders did not have a profitability reporting problem. Leadership could see which projects made or lost money once the work was complete.

The harder problem was timing. By the time margin performance became visible, the pursuit, pricing, scope, and cost-commitment decisions that shaped the outcome had already been made.

This engagement asked a more valuable question:

> **How can Bjerk recognize and control margin risk before construction begins, while improving the speed and visibility of the journey from lead to project start?**

Using Lean Six Sigma DMAIC, the engagement connected three views of the business that had previously been managed separately: pre-award flow, project-level financial performance, and operating controls. The analysis revealed a stable but under-capable margin process, concentrated delay in a small number of stages, and an early financial signal available at award.

The resulting operating model focused on three moves: create one measurable pursuit journey, remove avoidable decision queues, and introduce risk-based margin controls before award. The base-case model estimated **$940,000 in potential annual margin recovery at zero revenue growth**.

> **Important:** the financial impact is projected, not realized. It represents modeled value if the recommendations are implemented and perform as assumed.

## 1. The Business Was Managing Outcomes After the Point of Influence

Bjerk's existing data could answer a retrospective question: **Which completed projects underperformed?**

It could not reliably answer the questions leaders needed during the pre-award process:

- Where is an opportunity waiting, and who owns the next decision?
- Which pursuits require a different level of review?
- When are committed costs moving beyond the assumptions used to price the job?
- Is the project forecast changing as new information becomes available?
- Which interventions improve performance, and which simply add process?

Eight of nine pre-award stages lacked standardized timing. Project records were distributed across multiple systems and working practices. All 534 forecastable projects reviewed retained the original estimate as cost at completion, so the forecast was not functioning as a live indicator of emerging risk.

The business consequence was larger than a reporting gap. Bjerk was making consequential pursuit, pricing, and commitment decisions without a consistent operating view of flow or risk.

### The strategic reframing

The project began as a process-improvement question. The evidence reframed it as an operating-control question:

> **Bjerk needed to move visibility and decision control upstream, before project economics became difficult to change.**

That reframing guided the analysis and prevented the engagement from becoming a generic exercise in making every stage faster.

## 2. The Evidence Had an Important Constraint

The available data was strong enough to evaluate completed-project economics, but not strong enough to reconstruct actual historical stage-by-stage cycle time.

The analytical design treated those two evidence streams differently:

### What could be stated from actual results

- Margin performance and defect rates
- Project-level process capability
- Statistical stability after special-cause review
- Relationships between project characteristics and realized margin
- Scenario-based financial opportunity

### What required a planning basis

- Stage-level pre-award duration
- Waiting time by stage
- Current-state process-cycle efficiency
- Modeled cycle-time improvement

Rather than presenting estimated stage durations as historical facts, the planning ranges were used to prioritize where measurement and controlled pilots should begin.

**Why this mattered:** a senior recommendation is not only about what the data says. It is also about knowing what the data cannot support and designing the next decision around that limitation.

---

## 3. The Margin Problem Was Recurring, Not Exceptional

The first diagnostic tested whether margin underperformance was being driven mainly by a small number of unusual projects or whether it was embedded in normal performance.

The process capability analysis covered **428 completed projects**:

- **23.4%** finished below Bjerk's 10% margin target.
- **9.8%** finished below breakeven.
- The process operated at approximately **2.23 sigma**.
- The implied defect rate was **233,645 defects per million opportunities**.

After assignable-cause projects were removed through iterative control-chart review, no observations remained outside the revised limits. The process was statistically stable.

That finding changed the nature of the response. If a process is unstable, management investigates unusual events. If it is stable at an unacceptable level, management must change the underlying system.

> **Bjerk's margin problem was not random bad luck. The current process could predictably produce under-margin and margin-losing projects.**

![Process capability summary](02-process-capability.png)

### Decision implication

The appropriate response was not simply to review poor projects more closely after the fact. Bjerk needed earlier controls around the project types and decisions associated with margin exposure.

---

## 4. Growth Was Concentrating Exposure in the Riskiest Work

Segmenting performance by contract value exposed a second pattern.

- Projects below $100K had a **16.4%** rate below the margin floor.
- Projects from $100K to $500K had a **46.8%** rate below the floor.
- Projects from $500K to $1M had a **53.3%** rate below the floor.
- Projects of $1M or more also had a **53.3%** rate below the floor.

More than half of projects above $500K finished below the 10% margin requirement. The commercial risk was therefore not evenly distributed across the portfolio.

### Decision implication

A single pre-award path for every opportunity treated materially different risks as if they were the same. Larger, design-heavy, or subcontractor-heavy pursuits required stronger review thresholds, while smaller and lower-risk work could move through a faster path.

This became the basis for a tiered operating model rather than a one-size-fits-all process.

---

## 5. Most Delay Sat in a Small Number of Stages, but Not All of It Was Controllable

The pre-award journey was modeled across 13 stages. The Pareto analysis showed that **four stages accounted for approximately 81% of worst-case lead time**:

1. Owner design completion
2. Budget approval
3. Proposal-to-award
4. Subcontractor mobilization to project start

Owner design completion alone represented approximately **54%** of the modeled worst-case journey. However, it was primarily controlled by the owner, not Bjerk.

That distinction was essential. Treating all waiting as internal waste would have produced an unrealistic recommendation. The practical opportunity was twofold:

- Reduce internal decision queues in budget approval, proposal-to-award, and mobilization.
- Begin eligible estimating work on partial design sets instead of waiting for full design completion.

![Pareto analysis of pre-award lead time](03-cycle-time-pareto.png)

### The operating insight

Only about **5%** of modeled pre-award time was classified as pure non-value-added activity. Approximately **79%** was necessary non-value-added work such as design, approvals, reviews, setup, and mobilization.

The opportunity was therefore not broad cost cutting. It was orchestration: remove avoidable queues and run required work in parallel where risk allowed.

## 6. The Strongest Early Signal Appeared at Award

The most actionable relationship in the dataset was the gap between estimated and committed costs.

### Commitment-to-estimate gap vs. realized margin

**Pearson correlation: r = -0.67**  
**Explained variance: R-squared = 0.45**  
**Valid paired observations: n = 411**

As the commitment-to-estimate gap increased, realized margin tended to decline. Spearman correlation produced nearly the same result, reducing the likelihood that the relationship was driven only by outliers.

The analysis was observational, so it did not establish causation. Its value was operational: the indicator was available at award, before the final margin result was known.

### Decision implication

Bjerk could use the commitment-to-estimate gap as a leading review signal, especially for subcontractor-heavy pursuits. A threshold breach would not automatically stop a project. It would trigger a structured review of scope, self-perform assumptions, committed pricing, and required margin premium.

This converted a statistical relationship into a management control.

## 7. The Findings Pointed to One System, Not Seven Separate Fixes

Root-cause analysis, the Cause-and-Effect Matrix, and FMEA converged on the same conclusion: the business needed an integrated pre-award operating system.

The highest-priority causes included the absence of a standardized system of record, missing pursuit timestamps, static forecasting, inconsistent pricing controls, and a single journey for projects with different risk profiles.

The seven recommendations were organized into three mutually reinforcing layers.

### Layer 1: Visibility

Create one timestamped pursuit log with stage, owner, date-in, date-out, aging, project type, and forecast status. This becomes the common operating record for opportunity reviews, cycle-time measurement, and control monitoring.

### Layer 2: Flow

Move routine pursue/pass and estimator-assignment decisions to an asynchronous process below a defined threshold. Reserve the weekly meeting for exceptions. Pilot estimating on partial design sets when scope maturity and revision risk meet defined criteria.

### Layer 3: Margin discipline

Tier the journey by project complexity and risk. Introduce structured gates for subcontractor-heavy pursuits and change orders with incomplete scope or insufficient margin. Require senior review where exposure is highest.

![Recommendations organized around flow, margin discipline, and visibility](04-recommendations.png)

### Why the layers must work together

- Visibility without decision rules creates better reporting but no behavior change.
- Faster decisions without margin controls can accelerate poor work into the pipeline.
- Margin gates without reliable data become subjective and difficult to enforce.

The recommendation was therefore a connected management system: **make the work visible, route it according to risk, and intervene before award.**

## 8. The Business Case Was Built to Avoid Overclaiming

The financial model estimated the portion of historical margin leakage that stronger pre-award decisions could reasonably recover. Capture assumptions were applied by defect driver and capped at 70%.

### Projected annual margin recovery

- **Conservative:** $470K, approximately 1.0% of revenue
- **Base case:** $940K, approximately 2.1% of revenue
- **Optimistic:** $1.32M, approximately 2.9% of revenue

All scenarios assumed **zero revenue growth**.

Cycle-time benefits were deliberately excluded from the annual margin projection. Faster flow may create capacity to pursue more work, but that upside was not counted as booked financial value.

![Projected annual margin recovery by scenario](05-projected-business-impact.png)

> **The $940K base case is a projected opportunity, not realized savings, committed value, or guaranteed performance.**

This distinction matters. The business case was designed to support an implementation decision, not to make the analysis appear more successful than the evidence allowed.

## 9. The Future-State Decision Architecture

The recommendation redesigns how opportunities are governed before award. It does not add the same review burden to every project. Controls increase only as financial exposure, delivery complexity, or cost uncertainty increases.

### Entry decision: pursue, pass, or escalate

Routine opportunities below a defined risk threshold move through an asynchronous decision process with a named owner and response-time expectation. Exceptions, strategic pursuits, and incomplete opportunities move to leadership review.

**Required inputs**

- Client and opportunity profile
- Project type and estimated contract value
- Design maturity
- Delivery complexity
- Preliminary self-perform and subcontractor mix
- Strategic importance and known exceptions

**Control objective:** eliminate avoidable waiting without weakening pursuit discipline.

### Pricing decision: standard path or enhanced review

Opportunities are routed according to project size, design maturity, subcontractor exposure, and scope uncertainty.

**Enhanced review triggers**

- Contract value above the approved tier threshold
- Design-heavy work with incomplete documents
- Subcontractor-heavy delivery model
- Commitment-to-estimate ratio outside tolerance
- Change-order scope that is incomplete or difficult to validate
- Proposed margin below the approved floor

**Control objective:** apply senior attention where historical margin exposure is highest rather than slowing every pursuit.

### Award decision: proceed, reprice, mitigate, or decline

Before award, the project record should show the latest estimate, committed-cost position, margin expectation, key exclusions, unresolved scope, risk owner, and required mitigation.

**Control objective:** ensure that risk acceptance is explicit, documented, and visible before the organization commits to delivery.

### Transition to project start

A formal readiness check confirms that commercial terms, subcontractor coverage, project setup, ownership, and critical prerequisites are complete.

**Control objective:** prevent work from entering execution with unresolved pre-award gaps that later surface as cost, schedule, or margin problems.

## 10. Root Causes Were Prioritized by Their Effect on Business Outcomes

The root-cause work moved beyond brainstorming. Potential causes were evaluated against four weighted critical-to-quality outcomes: margin conformance, cycle time, measurability, and growth readiness.

### Highest-priority systemic causes

| Priority | Root cause | Business consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No standardized system of record | Weakens measurement, ownership, coordination, and auditability across the journey |
| 2 | No timestamped pursuit log | Prevents stage-level cycle-time measurement and aging visibility |
| 3 | Forecasts not updated | Removes the dynamic view needed to recognize emerging project risk |
| 4 | Change-order scope priced loosely | Increases exposure to scope gaps and margin leakage |
| 5 | Subcontractor-heavy pursuits without a commitment gate | Allows cost exposure to grow without a structured pricing response |
| 6 | One process for all project types | Applies the same controls to low-risk and high-risk opportunities |

FMEA reinforced the same pattern. The highest risk-priority numbers were concentrated in design completion, budget approval, proposal review and award, opportunity review, subcontractor bidding, and project-start readiness.

### Highest-priority failure modes

| Pre-award area | Failure mode | RPN | Management response |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Design completion | Documents delayed or incomplete | 270 | Design milestones, named owners, scheduled reviews, and escalation triggers |
| Budget approval | Budget rejected or repeatedly revised | 252 | Assumption review before submission and formal revision tracking |
| Proposal review and award | Proposal misaligned with scope, pricing, or requirements | 240 | Pre-submission review of pricing, scope, exclusions, and client requirements |
| Opportunity review | Pursue, assign, or budget decisions delayed | 224 | Rolling reviews with defined response-time expectations |
| Proposal development | Subcontractor bids late, incomplete, or not comparable | 210 | Standard bid packages, deadlines, and a coverage tracker |
| Project start | Work begins before prerequisites are complete | 210 | Formal readiness checklist and go or no-go decision |

The convergence across Pareto analysis, Cause-and-Effect prioritization, and FMEA increased confidence that the recommendations were addressing the vital few issues rather than isolated symptoms.

## 11. The Control Plan Converts Recommendations Into Management Measures

Implementation requires more than completing action items. Each recommendation needs an operational definition, owner, review cadence, and response when performance moves outside tolerance.

### Core management measures

| Measure | Definition | Purpose | Suggested review cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage aging | Calendar days since the opportunity entered its current stage | Identifies stalled opportunities and ownership gaps | Weekly |
| Decision turnaround | Time from complete decision request to pursue, pass, assign, or budget decision | Measures internal queue reduction | Weekly |
| Lead-to-award time | Days from qualified lead entry to award decision | Tracks overall pre-award flow | Monthly |
| Award-to-start time | Days from award to project start | Monitors mobilization and readiness | Monthly |
| Commitment-to-estimate ratio | Committed subcontractor cost divided by estimated subcontractor cost at award | Provides an early signal of margin exposure | At award and monthly |
| Forecast movement | Change in cost at completion and anticipated profit from the original estimate | Tests whether forecasting is functioning as a live control | Monthly |
| Margin defect rate | Share of completed projects below the 10% margin requirement | Confirms whether financial performance is improving | Monthly and quarterly |
| Gate compliance | Share of applicable pursuits completing the required review | Measures adoption of margin controls | Monthly |
| Pilot rework rate | Share of partial-design estimating work materially revised due to later design changes | Tests whether cycle-time gains create unacceptable rework | Per pilot and monthly |

### Escalation logic

- Opportunities exceeding the stage-aging threshold move to the accountable owner and process leader.
- Commitment-to-estimate ratios outside tolerance trigger scope, pricing, and subcontractor review.
- Forecasts with no movement despite material project changes require validation.
- Gate exceptions require a named approver, documented rationale, and follow-up review.
- Pilot work with excessive revision or rework is paused until eligibility criteria are adjusted.

### Control objective

The goal is not to create a larger reporting burden. The goal is to establish a small set of measures that make stalled work, margin exposure, exceptions, and adoption visible early enough for management action.

## 12. The Roadmap Sequenced Learning Before Scale

The recommendations were prioritized by expected impact, implementation effort, time to value, and dependency.

### Wave 1: Build the management foundation | Weeks 1 to 12

- Launch the timestamped pursuit log.
- Establish asynchronous decision rules and exception thresholds.
- Standardize project-type and forecast updates.
- Run a controlled partial-design estimating pilot.

Wave 1 creates the baseline needed to verify actual stage time, adoption, exceptions, and early financial signals.

### Wave 2: Add margin controls | One to two quarters

- Introduce the subcontractor-heavy pursuit gate.
- Add change-order scope and minimum-margin review.

These controls follow the measurement foundation so compliance and outcomes can be monitored rather than assumed.

### Wave 3: Redesign for scale | Two or more quarters

- Expand partial-design estimating where the pilot demonstrates value.
- Implement differentiated paths based on project complexity and risk.

The roadmap avoids a common transformation failure: scaling a new process before the organization can measure whether it works.

## What This Case Demonstrates

### Strategic problem framing

A broad request to improve the customer journey was reframed as a decision problem centered on when and how the business could influence margin.

### Analytical judgment

Completed-project actuals were used where the evidence was reliable. Missing timing data was disclosed, and modeled durations and financial opportunities were not presented as realized facts.

### Business translation

Technical findings were converted into management choices: which work should move faster, which work required stronger controls, what should trigger review, and what had to be measured before scaling.

### Implementation thinking

Quick wins, foundational data discipline, margin controls, and structural redesign were sequenced according to dependencies rather than treated as equally ready.

---

## Closing Perspective

The visible symptom was inconsistent margin performance. The deeper issue was that Bjerk's process made risk easiest to see after the organization had lost much of its ability to change the outcome.

The engagement moved the control point upstream.

By making the pursuit journey measurable, differentiating work by risk, and using pre-award financial signals to trigger review, Bjerk could manage margin as an operating process rather than a closeout result.
